Install

Set it up once on a computer you own. Then it lives on your phone.

Tacitus brings its own chat app. There is no chat service to sign up for and no account with anyone. You open it on your phone, add it to the Home Screen, and from then on it works wherever you are.

  1. Run the installer on an always-on Linux or Mac computer. It checks what is missing, installs Claude Code and opens setup.
  2. Answer setup from your phone or laptop. One screen per topic, in plain words, until the health check is green. It stops only for a password, a browser sign-in or your phone.
  3. Open the chat and add it to your Home Screen. It opens like an app. New cards, messages and alerts arrive as notifications while it is closed.
  4. Leave the house. Tap Set up this phone once. The phone makes its own keys, you approve it by matching a six-digit code, and the app checks every file it runs against your computer's signature.

You need

· A Claude Pro or Max subscription, or a ChatGPT subscription that includes Codex · A Microsoft 365 account and an app registration from your IT group (the longest wait) · Optionally, Google accounts for calendars and Gmail drafts · Optionally, a GPU and an open model to read the mail

On your phone

Installed like an app. Served by your own computer.

Tacitus serves its own chat from the computer it runs on. Add it to your Home Screen and it opens full screen, like any app. At home it reaches your computer over your private network. Anywhere else it goes through a sealed connection that nobody in the middle can read.

  • Notifications that clear themselves. A new card or message buzzes your phone. Answer the thread, and its notifications and unread count disappear on every device.
  • Needs you. One list at the top gathers every conversation that waits on you. Tap Mark done when one is finished, and the list shows only what is left.
  • Works on any network. Pair the phone once by matching a six-digit code. After that it works on mobile data and on any Wi-Fi.
  • Photos and files. Add a PDF or a photo of a form to a message and ask about it.

You getOne place on your phone for everything that waits on you, and a phone that goes quiet once you have answered.

Your approval

Nothing goes out until you tap.

A good assistant prepares, and you decide. Tacitus works the same way. Every email and every invite waits on a card with its button: Send, Create, Reschedule. You read it, change it if you want, and tap.

  • Your tap makes a one-time code. It works once, for 30 minutes. The model has no way to press the button or make the code.
  • The model runs as its own user on your computer and holds no key to your mailbox. A small program that runs as you does the reading, and it refuses to send.
  • The model cannot change the code it runs under.

You getThe work done ahead of you, and the last word on everything that leaves.

Inbox

One message. Ten drafts in your voice.

Go through the day's mail in one breath, the way you would brief a person. Each reply comes back drafted in the voice you use with that person, with meeting times where someone asked to meet. A tap on Send closes the item and files the thread.

  • It learns how you write to each person from 90 days of your sent mail: greeting, closing, length, formality.
  • A yes to a meeting always comes with two or three open times.
  • It keeps the list of what you owe, and an item closes itself when your reply goes out.

You getAn inbox cleared from your phone in two messages, with every reply in your own words.

Scheduling

Scheduling is a sentence.

Say who and roughly when. Tacitus reads your work and personal calendars, applies your rules and finds the times. It fills Doodle and When2Meet polls the same way. The invite waits on a Create button.

  • Your rules are recurring events on a calendar you already use: working hours, lunch, no-meeting Fridays, who comes first.
  • A request in your mail to move a meeting is spotted within minutes and staged as a button.
  • A solver finds the times on the real calendars, other people's included. The model never works out a free slot on its own.

You getA meeting placed from one sentence, inside the rules you set.

Letters, forms, spreadsheets

Dictate it. Get the signed PDF.

A recommendation letter from a voice memo. A form from a web link or a photo of the blank page. It fills them on your letterhead with your signature, checks every page, and tells you what it left blank.

  • It reads what the form asks for before it fills a field.
  • A letter keeps its source, so a revision changes the same letter.
  • Spreadsheets and Word files come back as files you open from the chat.
  • A longer working document can go up for private review on GitHub, where you comment on any line from your phone.

You getThe finished letter or form in the chat, ready to send.

Rooms for big projects

A room of its own, when you want one.

Most days, one conversation per request is enough. When a project grows, give it a room. The room keeps the project's people, mail and decisions, so its assistant already knows the file when you ask where things stand.

  • "Open a room for the spring fundraiser" opens one.
  • Drop in a call transcript and the plan updates.

Your rules

Say it once. In your own words.

When you state a preference, Tacitus writes it down in your words and follows it in every later briefing and draft. Your rules come ahead of its built-in habits.

  • Items close themselves from the evidence: your reply, a sent draft, a meeting on the calendar.
  • When the evidence is thin, it asks with a one-tap "Looks done?" card.
  • If you ever have to tell it that something is done, it logs that as its own mistake to fix.

You getAn assistant that learns a preference from one sentence and keeps it.

The weekly tip

One more thing, once a week.

Most people use an assistant like a chat box: ask, get an answer. Tacitus notices what you have not tried yet and, once a week, suggests one thing with one line to try. It never suggests something you already use.

  • Answer more for the next one, not now to pause for two weeks, or never to stop.
  • The phone tips wait until the app is on your phone. The room tip waits until you have had 20 conversations.

How it works

Five parts, in plain words.

An assistant that reads your mail has to earn the trust. Here is where everything runs, who sees what, and how it stays current. Each part links to the full document.

  1. Your computer

    Tacitus runs on one always-on Linux or Mac computer that you control. Your rules, the chat history, the list of what you owe and the keys to your mailbox stay there. No Tacitus service holds any of it.

    What stays on your machine

  2. The model you choose

    Claude Code or Codex does the judgment: the conversation, the hard reply, the plan. It sees the text a task needs. If you host an open model, it can read every email first, and a Settings screen picks which model runs each job. Plain code does the rest, such as reading the inbox and solving for meeting times, with no model at all.

    Model routing, job by job

  3. Signed updates that apply themselves

    Every release is signed. Your computer checks for one each hour, installs it when nothing is running, and confirms that it came up healthy. If it did not, the same job switches back and holds that version. You see one line about it in the next briefing.

    How updates work

  4. A sealed connection away from home

    Off your home network, the phone reaches your computer through a relay. The phone and your computer seal every message at each end, so the relay carries data it cannot open. It sees which install a message is for, its size and its time. The app checks its own code against your computer's signature before it runs.

    The relay, and what a dishonest one could and could not do

  5. Open source under the AGPL

    Every gate on this page is code you can read. The model runs as its own user, cannot read your mail keys, and cannot change its own code. A hosted relay is the one paid part, and you can run your own.
